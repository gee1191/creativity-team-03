HOW CERTIVITY'S EXPERTS WRITE A REQUIREMENT
(from requirement-writing standards: INCOSE Guide for Writing Requirements, NASA SE Handbook Appendix C,
ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148, the EARS templates; and measured on 94 requirements the same experts wrote from UN R175)

1. One sentence, one obligation, exactly one "shall" (or "shall not"). Never a fragment, a heading, a description,
   a definition or a bare permission. 99% of the experts' requirements contain "shall"; none is a "Heading: value".
2. An explicit subject that must comply, in active voice where the regulation allows: "The ACPE shall indicate
   ..." rather than "information shall be indicated". Resolve "it", "they", "these", "such lamps", and turn
   heading-style text into a sentence: "Vertical angle: 15° above and below the horizontal" becomes "The vertical
   angle of <the device> shall be 15° above and below the horizontal". About 60% of expert requirements start "The".
3. Conditions go first, in EARS form (about 35% of expert requirements):
   - state:       "While <state holds>, the <subject> shall ..."
   - event:       "When <event / trigger>, the <subject> shall ..."
   - feature:     "Where <the vehicle / device has feature X>, the <subject> shall ..."
   - failure:     "If <failure / unwanted event>, then the <subject> shall ..."
   - scope:       "For <vehicle category / test / device class>, the <subject> shall ..."
   Replace "in case of", "as soon as", "following", "during" by the matching keyword. Keep the regulation's own
   words for the condition itself.
4. Relaxations, exemptions and alternatives of the same obligation stay inside it, at the end: "..., except that
   <exception>" or "..., unless <condition>". A "However ...", "This requirement does not apply ...",
   "... may be reduced to 5°" or "Alternatively, at the choice of the manufacturer ..." sentence is NOT a requirement
   of its own: fold it into the requirement it relaxes.
5. Permissions ("may", "is permitted", "provided that") are not requirements by themselves. Either turn the
   condition into an obligation ("Lamps may be grouped ... provided that all requirements ... are fulfilled"
   becomes "While lamps are grouped ..., the lamps shall fulfil all requirements ..."), or fold the permission
   into the obligation it relaxes (rule 4). A permission that sets no obligation is dropped.
6. Keep the regulation's wording for the obligation itself: values, units, tolerances, angles, colours, table and
   paragraph references ("as specified in paragraph 5.4.3.", "Table 2b"), named standards. Restructure, do not
   paraphrase, shorten or round, and do not add anything the regulation does not say (no unit conversions, no
   explanations in brackets).
7. Still two obligations in one item (two "shall"s with different constraints, or a list)? Split it, repeating the
   subject and the condition in each part. Two items that state the same obligation? Keep one.
8. Duties of the manufacturer, applicant or authority to declare, justify, submit or notify are usually not written
   as product requirements unless they are the subject of the regulation itself.

9. Do not split one obligation into pieces. When the parts joined by "and", "or" or "and/or" describe the same
   obligation, keep them in one requirement:
   - paired qualities of the same thing: "The labels shall be durable and easily readable." is one requirement.
   - a number together with its approval or type: "The number of mirrors shall be two, approved as Class III
     devices according to Regulation No. 46." is one requirement.
   - several values or ranges of the same limit in one sentence: "The limit shall be 40 to 30 dB in the 10 to 50 MHz
     band and 30 to 35 dB in the 50 to 100 MHz band." is one requirement.
   - alternatives: "The plug shall be such that it cannot be fitted in any socket of type A and/or type B." is one
     requirement.
   Split only separate sentences, two different actions or results ("shall be fixed securely" / "shall not rattle"),
   and lists ((a), (b), (c), or a list of separate devices each given the obligation).
10. A case with its own obligation is its own requirement. When a paragraph gives the main rule and then a case that
   adds an obligation for another device or situation, write that case as a separate requirement that starts with
   Where / When / If and repeats what is required, in addition to the main rule. For example "However, where
   additional sensors are fitted, they shall be mounted at least 300 mm apart" becomes "Where additional sensors are
   fitted, the additional sensors shall be mounted at least 300 mm apart." The same holds for "when determining X,
   the area Y shall be excluded", "when the unit is combined with another function, it shall meet ...", and "in the
   case of type B, a check shall be made that ...". Fold a clause into the main rule with "except that" (rule 4) only
   when it relaxes or lifts the same obligation for the same subject ("... may be reduced to 5°", "this does not
   apply when ...").
11. Keep the regulation's own subject. When the sentence already names its subject ("the limits", "the markings",
   "the lamps"), keep that subject as written; do not add qualifiers the sentence does not have ("all", "broadband",
   "of the device", the product's name). Replace only pronouns and headings (rule 2).
